Ford Steps Into The Vehicle Telematics Space With SYNC Connect

For the past eight years, Ford has been notable in the vehicle connectivity space

for its emphasis on so-called “brought-in” solutions using its SYNC platform. Until now, SYNC has always relied on drivers connecting their phones via either Bluetooth or USB to provide services like 911 Assist and vehicle health reports. Starting in spring 2016, Ford is rolling out a new built-in cellular-connected telematics system dubbed SYNC Connect starting with the 2017 Escape.


Of course, telematics systems are nothing new, GM launched OnStar 20 years ago and most major automakers now offer systems that contain their own cellular radios that can communicate independently of a phone. Even Ford has a system called MyFord Mobile, but it has been exclusively available on plug-in vehicles including the Focus Electric, Fusion Energi and C-MAX Energi.


The downside of telematics systems that has often limited their use in the field is the need to subscribe to yet another paid service on top of everything else. In general, an estimated 25% of drivers continue to subscribe to telematics systems at a cost of $200 per year or more after initial trial periods run out.



The brought-in approach used for SYNC until now has always come at no additional incremental cost after whatever option cost was paid at the time of purchase because it relied on the driver’s phone for a connection. While this worked great for functions like calling emergency services in the event of an accident or sending vehicle health reports, it has limits. It only works when the phone is in the car and connected. Drivers that wanted to locate their car, lock or unlock the doors or remotely start it on a cold day were out of luck.


All of those capabilities are now made available through SYNC Connect. Ford has added a 4G LTE modem that uses the AT&T network to SYNC to provide this always-on, built-in connectivity that drivers can access from a smartphone app. Where Ford’s approach is different from other automakers is keeping SYNC Connect services free of charge, at least for the first five years.


For now, SYNC Connect offers fewer services and no call center capability which is where the real costs lie in telematics. That means you can’t call for turn-by-turn directions, get concierge services or have the vehicle slowed down if it gets stolen. “The potential is there to add additional services and features there in the future,” said Ford technology spokesman Alan Hall. No doubt those extra capabilities will become a new revenue stream for Ford.

Since the total amount of data transmitted over the LTE network with SYNC Connect is pretty minimal, it’s easy for Ford to build that cost into purchase price, knowing that users aren’t going to be torrenting movies using the connection. More importantly, having a cellular modem built-in to the vehicle means that Ford can now start planning for doing over-the-air software updates just like Tesla already does. Ford can already do OTA updates to SYNC 3 using WiFi when the car is connected at home.


Of course having that perpetual connection in the vehicle also means there is a new potential attack vector for hackers. Like other automakers, Ford has always declined to discuss what they are doing about automotive cybersecurity other than acknowledging that they take the issue seriously. Fortunately it appears that all automakers are now taking this issue far more seriously than they did a few years ago. Through the Alliance of Automobile Manufacturers and theAssociation of Global Automakers, OEMs are currently setting up the Automotive Information Sharing and Analysis Center to aid them in using best practices, find bugs and get them fixed before they can become a problem.
