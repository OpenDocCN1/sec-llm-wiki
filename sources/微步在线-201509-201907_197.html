A small market share and a trusted development environment protected Apple a long time, but will that last? Plus,EXCLUSIVE: more data on who's behind XCodeGhost.


A Series of Unfortunate Events

The past several months have been full of bad newsfor Mac and iOS. Here's a quick rundown of the highlights:

Keychain vulnerability: Reported to Apple a yearago, revealed to the public in June, and still not fixed, researchersdiscovered a vulnerability in Keychain on Mac OS X. Attackers could poisonKeychain and steal the data it stores, which included passwords and tokens fora variety of applications, including iCloud and Facebook.

Gatekeeper vulnerabilities: At the Black Hat Las Vegasconference in August, Synack director of research Patrick Wardle detailedproof-of-concept exploits that circumvent Gatekeeper, Apple's mechanism forpreventing unsigned code from running on Mac. At the Virus Bulletin Pragueconference in October, Wardle showed that Apple did not repair the problem withOS X El Capitan, released Sep. 30, and told Forbes that "Gatekeeper is noobstacle at all."

DYLD_PRINT_TO_FILE vulnerability: Discovered in July,patched in mid-August, this was a bug in an environment variable in Mac OS XYosemite that enabled root access.

Tpwn vulnerability: Publicly disclosed in mid-Augustbefore it was patched, Tpwn was a memory corruption bug in the kernel of OS XMavericks through Yosemite, that would allow local privilege escalation andgrant attackers root access.

KeyRaider: In late August, the KeyRaider iOS malwarestole 225,000 legitimate Apple accounts and slammed devices with ransomware,data theft, and phony purchases. The malware was secretly wrapped intounauthorized iOS apps, downloaded from a China-based third-party website, andthus it only affected jailbroken iOS devices.

AirDrop vulnerability: Disclosed in mid-September, avulnerability in both Mac and ioS -- patched in the new iOS 9 -- lets attackersbomb any iOS and Mac device within Bluetooth range with malware, via theAirdrop file-sharing feature.

XCodeGhost: In late September, attackers showed theycould hit non-jailbroken iOS devices too. XcodeGhost is a Trojanized version ofApple's application development software, Xcode. Attackers uploaded it toChinese cloud storage service Baidu Yunpan -- a regional, third-partyalternative to the Apple Store where download times are shorter for iOS and Macdevelopers in China. Innocent app developers then used XcodeGhost to write appsand upload them to the official App Store, never knowing that those apps weremalicious. Originally, it was thought that only about 40 apps were infectedwith XcodeGhost, but that number was later increased to 4,000, includingWeChat, ride-hailing app Didi Kuaidi, and music sharing app NetEase Music.

YiSpecter: In early October, researchers at Palo AltoNetworks discovered about 100 apps in the iTunes App Store abusing Apple'sprivate APIs -- used only by Apple itself and not available to app developers-- in order to circumvent the Store's security tools. YiSpecter download,install, and open applications, replace on-board apps with unwanted downloads,and force apps to show advertisements.

Yanked apps: Last week, Apple pulled some ad-blockingapps from its App Store after discovering that some of those apps installedroot certificates that expose all traffic, including encrypted traffic, fromthe device to the application. Apple is allowing the app developers to resubmitto the store after they make alterations.

Cracks in the Trusted Environment

Part 1: XCode Ghost

Of all the recent attacks on Apple operating systems,"XCodeGhost opened the door the widest," says Ryan Olson, director ofthreat intelligence for Unit 42, Palo Alto Networks.

It was noteworthy because it hit non-jailbroken iOSdevices and snuck weaponized apps onto Apple's official App Store. It crackedthe trust model that Apple security is based on.

Plus, "it showed that developers are a really softtarget," says Olson. Generally, security measures are constructed toprotect users from malicious app developers, not to protect app developers frommalware writers. XCodeGhost shows that these assumptions must no longer apply.

Researchers at ThreatBook Labs, a startup threatintelligence company based in China, believe they have uncovered moreinformation about the identity of the XCodeGhost authors and their intentions.

As researchers describe, Sep. 19, two days after themalware began attracting attention in China, someone claiming responsibilityfor the malware tweeted an apology, using the account @XcodeGhost-Author. Theysaid XcodeGhost was just a coding experiment to explore the potentialexploitation of a loophole in Xcode to enable advertisement delivery. They saidthat the only data they'd collected was device data -- which they'd sincedeleted -- and that the code's ad delivery capabilities had never been used andthat they'd taken their servers offline. Sep. 22 they deleted previous postspromoting downloads of their infected XCode IDE from the Unity3D discussionforum.

Hong Jia -- head of response and research of ThreatBookLabs and former principal research manager for Microsoft’s Malware ProtectionCenter -- isn't convinced the malware writers' apology is genuine.

"I don’t think they would ever have posted that'apology' letter if their actions hadn’t been exposed," says Jia. "Itseems they expect XcodeGhost to be lurking and plan to use it for a long time.The sudden exposure surprised them and they want to mitigate the panic andsecurity team’s attention."

Part of what indicates to Jia that XCodeGhost was not soinnocent, is the length of time it was being worked on -- over seven months. Asshe explains, the XCodeGhost team registered its first domain,"icloud-analysis.com" on Feb. 25, by which time, Jia says they werealready prepared to deploy or at least conduct end-to-end tests of the malware.Over the next seven months, they registered more domains -- over 20 new domainsduring August and September alone. They had at least three command-and-controlservers, icloud-analysis.com, icloud-diagnostics.com, and crash-analytics.com,all registered by an anonymous user. Based on this behavior, says Jia, "wecan see the XcodeGhost team attempts to poison more different Xcode packages toincrease XcodeGhost spreading."

The apology said that all XCodeGhost was doing wascollecting device information. However, as researchers at Palo Alto describedit, the malware could also issue fake alert messages to social engineer usersinto entering credentials, "hijack opening specific URLs based on theirscheme, which could allow for exploitation of vulnerabilities in the iOS systemor other iOS apps," and "read and write data in the user’s clipboard,which could be used to read the user’s password if that password is copied froma password management tool."

"The stealthy functions implemented in XcodeGhost isfar beyond what 'apology' letter says," says Jia. She suggests that itsfull functions might have been actively used if the malware hadn't beenexposed. "The XcodeGhost team’s plan seems be interrupted."

As for the team itself, ThreatBook Labs estimates thereare three to five people working on XcodeGhost, with different individualsworking on development of functionality, distribution of the poisoned Xcodepackage, and promotion of the package in Internet forums.

One of these individuals is probably a university studentlocated in Shandong province. By analyzing historical IP data of the command-and-controlservers, researchers found other domains that resolved to the same IPs that theXCodeGhost C2 servers once resolved to. Those domains had a common registrant,and by correlating identity data from other sources, they discovered a QQnumber (for an instant messaging service) registered to a university student inShandong.

A key reason XCodeGhost succeeded was because many iOSapp developers in China download Xcode from third-party services, instead ofdirectly from Apple. Jia explains the scale of the problem. "The wholeXcode package is about 4G," she says, "it takes about two hours foriOS app developers in China to download it. If they download it from BaiduCloud, it takes about 20 mins. This is a big advantage for iOS App developers.

"I don’t think XcodeGhost incident will stop iOS appdeveloper to stop using these third-party services," says Jia."However after XcodeGhost incident, iOS app developer may be more cautiouswhen they download from third-party service, like they will examine the package’sintegrity by checking package’s HASH and digital signature. If Apple wants toenhance its security model in China, I think they should increase CDN in Chinato increase the developer’s user experience."

Cracks in the Trusted Environment

Part 2: Gatekeeper Vulnerabilities

The other core part of Apple's trusted environment isthat all code that runs on Mac and iOS (non-jailbroken ones that is) must besigned. Not only does that prevent unofficial third-party apps from running,but it's supposed to prevent the execution of malware that might sneak in fromother places. That's essentially the domain of Apple Gatekeeper.

"Gatekeeper has one job," says Synack's Wardle,"and that is to block unsigned code from the Internet."

However, as Wardle has shown over the past few months,Gatekeeper is not doing its job very well. At the Black Hat Las Vegasconference in August, he showed several proof-of-concept exploits for Mac OS Xthat circumvent Gatekeeper.

Then, at the Virus Bulletin Prague conference in October,Wardle showed that Apple did not repair the problem with OS X El Capitan,released Sep. 30. Plus, he showed a new way of exploiting it.

Wardle snuck unsigned malicious code past Gatekeeper bywrapping it into a signed installer package. Gatekeeper only checks theinstaller package, not what's in it -- so it's vulnerable to what isessentially a basic piggybacking attack that any good lesson in socialengineering cautions against.

This latest proof-of-concept inspired Wardle to tellForbes that "Gatekeeper is no obstacle at all."

Mac Malware Rising

Research released today by Bit9 + Carbon Black ThreatResearch team found that five times more OS X malware have appeared in 2015already than during the previous five years combined.

Over a 10-week period they analyzed "more than 1,400unique samples taken from the Bit9 + Carbon Black Threat Research Team’sresearch engagements, open sources, and from incident response engagementsinvolving OS X, peer research, black lists, and contagio malware dump."Mike Sconzo, senior threat researcher at Bit9 + Carbon Black says this is"near-exponential growth," and expects to see that rate of growthcontinue.

Sconzo says about half of the malware they detected wasadware, but they have also noticed an increase in sophisticated malware and heexpects a wider variety of commodity malware on the horizon.

Given OS X’s roots in FreeBSD, researchers expected tosee more UNIX- or LINUX-based malware, but they found little to none of it.Sconzo says they've speculated this might mean that most Mac malware writersare new to writing malware for Mac -- perhaps having come from a Windowsmalware writing background or being new to writing malware altogether.

(This is an intriguing observation when paired with anobservation Palo Alto's Olson made last November when the WireLurker malwarehit the scene, which Olson said seemed to be written by people who were veryexperienced Mac developers, but not experienced virus writers.)

Can't Hide Behind Small Market Share

When one operating system commands 90 percent of themarket, attackers who value their own time focus their efforts on that oneoperating system, not the other puny ones. That's another key reason nearly allmalware has been aimed at Windows over the years, and Macs have remainedlargely unscathed.

"[Apple] got to hide behind the fact they weren't inthe major market share," says Sconzo.

The popularity of iOS, however, has drastically changedthe situation. According to Net Market Share's September figures, while Macoperating systems only claimed 7.72 percent of the global desktop OS marketshare, iOS claimed 38.6 percent of the global mobile OS market share. Thenumber of iOS devices in the enterprise might actually be higher. According toGood Technology's Q2 Mobility Index Report iOS had 64 percent of worldwideenterprise market share, although this had dropped from 70 percent the previousquarter.

As mobile devices become more critical components ofenterprise technology, more valuable business data will reside upon them.

"Attackers go where the data is," says Olson."The more data moves to those devices, I fully expect the attackers tofollow them."

Greater reliance on mobile devices, may cause a greaterneed for better integration with desktop machines. If iOS is the handheld ofchoice, it could eventually push a wider enterprise use of Mac (although thishas been predicted, and not seen, for years).

"[Mac] is maybe on the cusp of becoming a morepopular enterprise operating system," says Sconzo. He advises Mac housesto start approaching security in the same strategic way Windows houses do,instead of assuming they're safe because they use Mac.

The only trouble is, when trying to secure themselves,Mac houses don't have the same defenses at their disposal.

Mac Security Tools Aren't Fully Grown

As Sconzo explains, since the enterprise market sharewasn't in Apple, the enterprise attackers weren't there, and the enterpriseendpoint security tools weren't there. Now that Apple does have a greatermarket share, the attackers will follow, and they may get there before theendpoint security tools catch up.

Another factor, which is both a blessing and a curse, isthat the majority of Mac malware is relatively unsophisticated.

"The problem is," says Wardle, "becauseMac malware is so amateur, the third-party detection tools are similarlyamateur." This is part of the reason Wardle began writing craftier malwarefor Mac: to raise the bar.

Compounding the problem, paradoxically, is the very trustmodel that Apple uses to secure itself.

As Wardle explains, "It's basically their way or thehighway." Apple tightly limits the users' and applications' visibilityinto and control over the underlying system. The trouble with this is the usershave to put all their trust in Apple's ability to keep attackers out; they haveno way of digging around and finding out for themselves if a baddie hasbreached the defenses. While this may be good enough for basic home users, itmay not be good enough to protect enterprise users from sophisticatedattackers.

"The reality is," says Wardle, "it willprotect naïve users from lame attackers."

The good news, says Wardle, is that his experience withApple seems to indicate that the company is taking security more seriously. Hesays they are working more quickly, devoting more dedicated resources to fixingproblems, and have an intelligent, responsive internal security staff who workclosely with him whenever he reports a bug.

"My concern is more that they're not baking securityinto the development process," he says. In comparison to Microsoft, whichfollows a very detailed secure software development lifecycle, says Wardle,"I don't get that same vibe from Apple. Hopefully they're working towardsthat."

Still Way Better Than Android

Olson says the fact that every single Apple security flubis news, shows that Apple is still better than the rest.

"All of these [events] have been shaking people'sconfidence a little," says Olson. For years some people were led tobelieve that as long as you used Apple you were safe, he says, but while thatmight not be the case and suggesting that iOS is impenetrable would give you afalse sense of confidence, "you're still in a pretty good position."

Although recent events have shown that the Apple systemof only running signed code can be circumvented, Olson says that the systemstill gives Apple machines a step up on Android. While Apple users must undergoa complicated process to jailbreak their phones, Android users just need toflip a switch to accept third-party code. "That's the biggest reason ofthem all that Androids get hacked more ... Apple's never going to add thatswitch."

"I think Apple security is still better thanAndroid," says Jia. "However Apple’s strictly checked closed systemis not the silver bullet with which to get iOS app safe from threats whichApple hope it does."

Wardle points out that if writing exploits for iOS waseasy, an intelligence agency contractor wouldn't have offered a whopping $1million bounty for a remote iOS 9 exploit last month.

"I think Apple's done a really good job securingiOS," says Wardle. "I can't say the same for Mac."

Yet even despite the unfixed Gatekeeper flaws in OS X,Wardle says "The sky is not falling. I'm not sure Macs will ever be reallyinsecure."

The threats will only get more intense, but Apple knows"they're in that big of a battle," says Olson, "and they'repreparing for it."
